MATHEMATICAL FOUNDATIONS · LECTURE NOTES 01

Multivariate
calculus.

Differential calculus describes the local response of a function to changes in its inputs. This chapter extends the derivative from scalar functions to functions of several variables, and then develops the chain rule and the Jacobian matrix.

CHAPTER OUTLINE
  1. Scalar and vector variables
  2. Partial derivatives and gradients
  3. Derivatives of linear maps
  4. The multivariate chain rule
  5. Jacobians and local linearization

All derivatives are written using the numerator-layout convention.

01

ONE INPUT

The univariate derivative

For a scalar-valued function of one variable, the derivative measures the local rate of change of the output with respect to the input. It is a local quantity and generally varies with the point at which it is evaluated.

Scalar and vector variables

A scalar variable \(x\) is specified by one real number. A vector variable \(\mathbf{x}\) consists of an ordered collection of real-valued coordinates.

Vector-valued inputs arise naturally in machine learning. An image may be represented by a vector of pixel values, while the parameters of a model may be represented by a vector of weights. The move from \(x\) to \(\mathbf{x}\) therefore allows several input variables to be treated as a single mathematical object.

\(x \in \mathbb{R}\)scalar
\(\mathbf{x} = [x_1,x_2,\ldots,x_m]^{\mathsf T} \in \mathbb{R}^m\)vector
DEFINITION OF THE DERIVATIVE

Let \(y=f(x)\). For a nonzero finite increment \(h\), the difference quotient gives the average rate of change over an interval. The derivative is obtained by taking the limit as the length of that interval approaches zero.

\[\frac{\mathrm{d}y}{\mathrm{d}x}=\lim_{h\to0}\frac{f(x+h)-f(x)}{h}.\]

For \(f(x)=6x^2-3x+2\), differentiation gives \(f'(x)=12x-3\). Since the derivative depends on \(x\), the local rate of change is different at different points of the domain.

EXERCISE

Determine the value of \(x\) at which \(f'(x)=0\), and interpret the sign of \(f'(x)\) on either side of this point.

SUM

\((f+g)'=f'+g'\)

PRODUCT

\((fg)'=f'g+fg'\)

QUOTIENT

\(\left(\frac{f}{g}\right)'=\frac{f'g-fg'}{g^2}\)

CHAIN

\((g\circ f)'(x)=g'(f(x))f'(x)\)

The sum, product, and quotient rules describe the derivatives of standard algebraic combinations of functions. The chain rule describes the derivative of a composition. It is especially important in neural networks, which are constructed by composing a sequence of parameterized transformations.

02

MANY INPUTS

Partial derivatives and gradients

A scalar function of \(m\) variables may change differently with respect to each coordinate. Its local behavior is therefore described by \(m\) partial derivatives rather than by a single scalar derivative.

Definition of a partial derivative

Let \(f:\mathbb{R}^m\to\mathbb{R}\). The partial derivative with respect to \(x_i\) is defined by varying \(x_i\) while holding all other coordinates fixed.

\[\frac{\partial f}{\partial x_i}=\lim_{h\to0}\frac{f(x_1,\ldots,x_i+h,\ldots,x_m)-f(\mathbf{x})}{h}.\]

If \(f\) is differentiable at \(\mathbf{x}\), the collection of its partial derivatives describes the first-order sensitivity of the output to every coordinate. A component of large magnitude indicates strong local sensitivity to the corresponding input. A zero component indicates that the output has no first-order variation in that coordinate direction.

GEOMETRIC INTERPRETATION

Under the numerator-layout convention, \(\nabla_{\mathbf{x}}f\) is a row vector. Its transpose, \((\nabla_{\mathbf{x}}f)^{\mathsf T}\), gives the direction of steepest local increase. The entries are the slopes measured along the coordinate axes.

GRADIENT CONVENTIONS
NUMERATOR LAYOUT · USED HERE
\[\nabla_{\mathbf{x}}f=\begin{bmatrix}\frac{\partial f}{\partial x_1}&\frac{\partial f}{\partial x_2}&\cdots&\frac{\partial f}{\partial x_m}\end{bmatrix}\]
1 × m row vector
DENOMINATOR LAYOUT
\[\begin{bmatrix}\frac{\partial f}{\partial x_1}\\[2pt]\frac{\partial f}{\partial x_2}\\ \vdots\\ \frac{\partial f}{\partial x_m}\end{bmatrix}\]
m × 1 column vector

The two conventions contain the same partial derivatives but use different orientations. This difference affects the order of subsequent matrix products. Numerator layout is used throughout these notes.

03

DERIVATIVES AS MAPS

Derivatives of linear functions

The derivative of a nonlinear function generally varies across its domain. A linear map acts on displacements in the same manner at every point, and its derivative is therefore constant. More generally, the derivative of a differentiable function provides a local linear approximation at each point.

SCALAR
\[\frac{\partial x}{\partial x}=1\]
LINEAR FUNCTION
\[\frac{\partial(mx+c)}{\partial x}=m\]
QUADRATIC
\[\frac{\partial x^2}{\partial x}=2x\]
COORDINATE PROJECTION
\[\frac{\partial x_1}{\partial\mathbf{x}}=\begin{bmatrix}1&0&\cdots&0\end{bmatrix}\]
WORKED EXAMPLE

Matrix-vector multiplication

Consider \(\mathbf{f}(\mathbf{x})=A\mathbf{x}\). Its \(i\)-th output is \(f_i(\mathbf{x})=\sum_{j=1}^{m}a_{ij}x_j\). Consequently, the partial derivative of \(f_i\) with respect to \(x_j\) is \(a_{ij}\).

Computing this derivative for every pair of input and output coordinates reproduces the entries of \(A\). Hence the derivative of the linear map is the matrix that defines the map.

RESULT\(\displaystyle \frac{\partial(A\mathbf{x})}{\partial\mathbf{x}}=A\)

A derivative may therefore be interpreted as a linear transformation from infinitesimal input displacements to infinitesimal output displacements. For nonlinear functions this transformation depends on the evaluation point; for the linear function above it is constant.

EXERCISE

If \(A\) has shape \(p\times m\), determine the dimensions of the input, the output, and the derivative.

04

COMPOSED FUNCTIONS

The multivariate chain rule

Suppose that a scalar parameter \(t\) affects a function \(f\) through intermediate variables. The total derivative of \(f\) with respect to \(t\) must account for the dependence of each intermediate variable on \(t\).

ONE PARAMETER AND TWO INTERMEDIATE VARIABLES

Let \(f=f(x_1,x_2)\), where both \(x_1\) and \(x_2\) are functions of \(t\).

The contribution through \(x_1\) is the product of \(\partial f/\partial x_1\) and \(\mathrm{d}x_1/\mathrm{d}t\). The corresponding contribution through \(x_2\) is obtained in the same manner. The total derivative is the sum of these contributions.

\[\frac{\mathrm{d}f}{\mathrm{d}t}=\frac{\partial f}{\partial\mathbf{x}}\frac{\mathrm{d}\mathbf{x}}{\mathrm{d}t}=\begin{bmatrix}\frac{\partial f}{\partial x_1}&\frac{\partial f}{\partial x_2}\end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix}\frac{\mathrm{d}x_1}{\mathrm{d}t}\\[2pt]\frac{\mathrm{d}x_2}{\mathrm{d}t}\end{bmatrix}\]
\[=\frac{\partial f}{\partial x_1}\frac{\mathrm{d}x_1}{\mathrm{d}t}+\frac{\partial f}{\partial x_2}\frac{\mathrm{d}x_2}{\mathrm{d}t}.\]

Each path of dependence contributes a product of derivatives.

The sum over these paths is represented by matrix multiplication.

1 × 2×2 × 1=1 × 1
EXAMPLE

Let \(f(x_1,x_2)=x_1^2+2x_2\), where \(x_1=\sin t\) and \(x_2=\cos t\). There are two paths of dependence from \(t\) to \(f\), one through each intermediate variable.

\[\frac{\mathrm{d}f}{\mathrm{d}t}=\underbrace{2x_1\cos t}_{t\to x_1\to f}\;-\;\underbrace{2\sin t}_{t\to x_2\to f}=2\sin t\,(\cos t-1).\]

The negative sign in the second term follows from \(\mathrm{d}(\cos t)/\mathrm{d}t=-\sin t\).

MULTIPLE PARAMETERS

If \(\mathbf{x}\) depends on the vector \(\mathbf{s}=[s,t]^{\mathsf T}\), the derivative must describe the effect of each component of \(\mathbf{s}\). In numerator layout, this relation is expressed by the following matrix product.

\[\frac{\partial f}{\partial\mathbf{s}}=\frac{\partial f}{\partial\mathbf{x}}\frac{\partial\mathbf{x}}{\partial\mathbf{s}}\]
05

MANY INPUTS, MANY OUTPUTS

The Jacobian matrix

For a function with \(m\) inputs and \(n\) outputs, the first-order dependence of every output on every input is represented by an \(n\times m\) matrix of partial derivatives. This matrix is called the Jacobian.

Rows and columns of the Jacobian

Let \(\mathbf{f}(\mathbf{x})=[f_1(\mathbf{x}),f_2(\mathbf{x}),\ldots,f_n(\mathbf{x})]^{\mathsf T}\in\mathbb{R}^n\). The entry \(J_{ij}\) gives the local rate of change of output \(f_i\) with respect to input \(x_j\).

\[J=\nabla_{\mathbf{x}}\mathbf{f}=\frac{\partial\mathbf{f}}{\partial\mathbf{x}},\qquad J_{ij}=\frac{\partial f_i}{\partial x_j}.\]

The \(i\)-th row contains the derivatives of one output with respect to all inputs. The \(j\)-th column contains the derivatives of all outputs with respect to one input. These interpretations follow directly from the numerator-layout convention.

\[J=\begin{bmatrix}\frac{\partial f_1}{\partial x_1}&\frac{\partial f_1}{\partial x_2}&\cdots&\frac{\partial f_1}{\partial x_m}\\[4pt]\frac{\partial f_2}{\partial x_1}&\frac{\partial f_2}{\partial x_2}&\cdots&\frac{\partial f_2}{\partial x_m}\\ \vdots&\vdots&\ddots&\vdots\\[2pt]\frac{\partial f_n}{\partial x_1}&\frac{\partial f_n}{\partial x_2}&\cdots&\frac{\partial f_n}{\partial x_m}\end{bmatrix}_{n\times m}\]
ROWS CORRESPOND TO OUTPUTS · COLUMNS CORRESPOND TO INPUTS
LOCAL LINEARIZATION

The geometric interpretation

In a sufficiently small neighborhood of a point \(\mathbf{x}\), a differentiable nonlinear function is approximated by a linear transformation. The Jacobian is the matrix of this transformation. Depending on the function and evaluation point, it may stretch, contract, rotate, or eliminate particular directions.

For a sufficiently small displacement \(\Delta\mathbf{x}\), the corresponding output displacement is approximated by

\[\Delta\mathbf{f}\approx J(\mathbf{x})\,\Delta\mathbf{x}.\]

Summary

The gradient describes the first-order dependence of one scalar output on several inputs. The Jacobian extends this construction to several outputs. The chain rule composes the corresponding local linear transformations. Backpropagation applies this composition efficiently to the sequence of transformations that constitutes a neural network.
